DNA synthesis without chemistry.

Ordering a gene takes a week. All of biotech has to work around that wait.
The chemistry behind it has not changed in forty years.

We use an enzyme and switch it on and off with light, so gene synthesis is on demand and the only running costs are photonics upkeep and the DNA itself rather than the four-step chemical cycle our competitors require.

Light is faster than chemistry

TdT is the enzyme that adds DNA letters to the end of a strand. Left alone, it adds random letters of DNA uncontrollably, so the entire field is trying to make it stop after just one.

We attach a light-sensitive molecule next to the active site. In its resting state, it reaches the site and blocks it, preventing random addition. One flash of light bends the molecule into an unfavourable orientation to the active site; it is now too short to block, and the enzyme adds a letter. A second flash triggered by incorporation straightens it, closing the site again until the next intended base arrives.

The central problem in building DNA with an enzyme is that incorporation is stochastic: the enzyme adds a base when it happens to, and the waiting time is random.

If you cannot see and control in real time what each site is doing, the only option is a fixed window: one clock for the whole chip, set at about the mean incorporation time. With a well-chosen window the average site does add exactly one base, but the average is not the distribution: in any window some sites add nothing and others add two or more. No choice of window fixes that, and the best a single clock can do is get about a third of sites right. That error compounds with every base, so most of the work moves downstream, into finding and verifying the few molecules that came out right. Or what most of our competitors do: use chemical caps and extensive wash cycles to try to tame stochastic incorporation, importing the cost structure of chemical synthesis onto their enzymatic method.

Same chip, same enzyme. The open-loop limit is fixed by the waiting-time statistics and no choice of window moves it. The closed-loop limit is not statistical: it is set by how often the switch leaks while shut, which is an engineering number. The bar shows our design target for the first construct.

We overcome that limitation with light. A zero-mode waveguide is a hole narrow enough to watch one enzyme at a time, and it sees each base land. The instant a base goes in at one site, that site’s light goes out and the molecule folds back over the site. No site waits on the mean, because every site runs on its own.

In other words, we read every base of DNA that we write, and use every successful incorporation to queue the next.

Every other way of building DNA with an enzyme spends something on every base. A terminator rides on the nucleotide and has to be stripped off by chemistry. A tethered polymerase is itself the stop signal, and is released from the product each cycle. Either way the thing doing the controlling is used up once per base, and that sets the cycle time, the yield ceiling and the reagent bill. Our molecule is only switched. It survives every cycle, and what the machine consumes is the building blocks and the light. Its limits are decoupled from the chemistry: what bounds it instead is instrument latency and photocycle count.

Latency is solved engineering, not an open question. The instrument is what we are going to build.

The number is the mean distance of the two carboxylate oxygens from the catalytic magnesium, read from all-atom molecular dynamics of both isomers. Each panel puts the carboxylate at the distance that trajectory gives it, so the E trajectory moves 0.03 nm over the run and the Z trajectory 0.53 nm, which is what the simulation says and all it says. In E the carboxylate is chelating, so it sits symmetrically on the ion and the two oxygens are equidistant; in Z it is not, so it is free to tilt and the two distances differ, which is why the number is their mean. The photoswitch is drawn to real azobenzene bond lengths and angles; R stands for the rest of the ligand, which the geometry here does not turn on.
